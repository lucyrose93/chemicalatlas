# Chemical Atlas — Breakout Group Analyses

## Group 1: Critical Minerals

### 🔑 Key Themes

- **Transdisciplinary Audiences**  
  Emphasis on engaging diverse but specific audiences across disciplines.

- **Sources of Knowledge**  
  Integration of scientific knowledge with artistic, historical, and experiential insights.

- **Geopolitical Framing**  
  Tracing extractive processes through global supply chains, from sites of mining (e.g. China) to policy hubs (e.g. Geneva).

- **Visual Narrative Structures**
  - Mapping flows: mine → product → waste
  - Overlaying extractive histories with current data
  - Combining historical maps, geochemical data, and vignettes

### 🧠 Conceptual Highlights

- **"Atlas + Coloniality"**  
  Exploration of how mapping can reveal or reinforce colonial legacies in resource extraction.

- **Flow-Based Visual Logic**  
  Movement and transformation — rather than static territory — as key to visualising critical minerals.

- **Interrogating the Form**  
  Posing foundational questions such as: *Why an atlas? What does it allow or limit?*

### 🧭 Design Implications

- Incorporate multiple knowledge sources in each entry (scientific, historical, visual).
- Use the flow of materials as a structuring narrative.
- Include visual references to global power structures and trade routes.
- Develop map forms that reflect movement, not just location.

## Group 2: Plastics + Public Engagement

### 🔑 Key Themes

- **Target Groups**  
  Focus on engaging schools, journalists, communities, and researchers.

- **Modes of Engagement**
  - Interactive narratives (e.g. “choose your own adventure”)
  - Use of metaphor and humour (e.g. pancakes, food chains)
  - Emotional and moral framing: curiosity, anxiety, responsibility

- **Chemical Life Cycles**  
  Tracing plastics from production to disposal, and their transformation into "safe" or "compliant" forms.

- **Questions of Agency**  
  Who is responsible? What is made visible or invisible in the lifecycle?

### 👥 Collaborative Network

- Personal and institutional actors (e.g. researchers and educators) highlighted in node-like structures.
- Suggests an authorship model that is distributed and recognises contributors by role.

### 🧭 Design Implications

- Build emotionally resonant storytelling modes.
- Include narrative branches based on reader decisions or roles.
- Represent regulatory ambiguity and contested "safety" visually.
- Allow multi-perspective navigation (e.g. by profession, exposure, interest).

## Group 3: PFAS + Chemical Classification

### 🔑 Key Themes

- **PFAS as a Hyperobject**  
  Over 14,000 individual compounds; highly persistent and widespread.

- **Communication Challenges**
  - Complexity in classification
  - Low public understanding
  - Regulatory gaps and inconsistencies

- **Need for New Representational Tools**  
  Focus on improving how we communicate invisible, long-term, or uncertain chemical effects.

### 🧰 Proposed Functions for the Atlas

- **Atlas of Methodologies**  
  Visualise how we study, classify, and attempt to govern chemicals, not just what they are.

- **Audience-Centric Navigation**
  Tools for different knowledge levels — from expert researchers to the general public.

- **Scalability of Interface**
  A flexible interface that supports:
    1. Distant overview
    2. Project-level stories
    3. Personal or community impacts

### 🧭 Design Implications

- Treat the atlas as a *scaffold* for understanding, not just a dataset.
- Make classification systems visible and explorable.
- Offer layered entry points (e.g. by health effect, location, regulatory status).
- Support searching by uncertainty, not only by certainty.
